# Business Storytelling

Turn business content into a story that moves an audience to act, using proven frameworks.

## Discovery first

Before you choose a framework, you need five answers:

1. **Format:** presentation, document, email, pitch deck or case study.
2. **Audience:** executives, prospects, the team, investors or the general public.
3. **Action:** what they should do afterwards, such as approve, buy, change behaviour, understand or share.
4. **Current state:** unaware, sceptical, interested or ready to act.
5. **Time:** 30 seconds, 5 minutes, 30 minutes, or a written piece.

Take what the user already told you. Ask only for what is missing, in one message. If nobody can answer, for example when you run as a subagent or the user says "just write it", don't ask. Assume a sceptical audience that has to decide something, and the format the material suggests. Name your assumptions in one line after the result.

## Choose a framework

Pick one framework for the structure. Use SUCCESs on top of it as a checklist, not as a structure.

| The audience needs to... | Typical audience | Framework | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Act now | Ready, but needs a push | Monroe's Motivated Sequence | Sales pitches, calls to action |
| Buy into a vision or change | Sceptical, or comfortable with the status quo | Duarte Sparkline | Keynotes, vision and change presentations |
| Decide fast | Senior, executive | SCQA, answer first | Executive summaries, proposals, consulting |
| See themselves in the story | Prospects, case study readers | Hero's Journey | Case studies, customer and brand stories |
| Remember the message | Anyone | SUCCESs checklist | Every story, after you chose a structure |

Say in one line which framework you chose and why.

## Frameworks

### Monroe's Motivated Sequence

For driving action. Five steps:

1. **Attention:** a hook, such as a surprising fact, a question or a short story.
2. **Need:** the problem. Make it felt, not just known.
3. **Satisfaction:** your solution.
4. **Visualization:** the future if they act, or the future if they don't.
5. **Action:** one specific, clear next step.

### Duarte Sparkline

For presentations that inspire change. Alternate between:

- **What is** → **what could be** → **what is** → **what could be**
- End with the **new bliss**: the transformed future.

The contrast creates tension and momentum. Each "what could be" should be more compelling than the last.

### SCQA (Minto)

For executive communication. Four parts:

- **Situation:** context everyone agrees on.
- **Complication:** what changed, or what the problem is.
- **Question:** the question this raises.
- **Answer:** your recommendation.

For senior audiences, start with the answer and use situation, complication and question as support. Keep the situation to one or two sentences, so it doesn't become background.

### SUCCESs (Heath brothers)

A checklist to make ideas stick:

- **Simple:** the core message fits in one sentence.
- **Unexpected:** it breaks a pattern or opens a curiosity gap.
- **Concrete:** specific details instead of abstractions.
- **Credible:** details, numbers or claims the audience can test.
- **Emotional:** it appeals to identity or self-interest.
- **Stories:** a narrative, not just data.

### Hero's Journey (simplified)

For case studies and transformation stories:

1. **Ordinary world:** the situation before.
2. **Call to adventure:** the problem or opportunity.
3. **Resistance:** first doubts or obstacles.
4. **Meeting the mentor:** help arrives, such as your product or service.
5. **Transformation:** the change itself.
6. **Return with the elixir:** the measurable outcome.

The customer is the hero, not your company.

## Story elements

Every business story needs:

- **Stakes:** what is at risk, and why anyone should care.
- **Conflict:** what stands in the way. Without conflict there is no story.
- **Specificity:** names, numbers and details. Not "a company" but "a 50-person logistics firm in Rotterdam".
- **Transformation:** what changed from beginning to end.

## Facts

Specific details make a story credible, so they must be true. Use only names, numbers, quotes and results that the user gave you or that you can point to in a source. Never invent a statistic, a customer, a quote or an outcome to make the story stronger. If a detail is missing, write a visible placeholder such as `[number of customers]` or `[quote from the customer]`, and list the placeholders after the result. Comparisons that make a number human, such as "one customer every 12 seconds", must follow from the real number.

## Opening

Open with one of these, never with company history or background:

- a surprising fact;
- a provocative question;
- a moment of tension ("Last Tuesday, everything changed...");
- a counterintuitive statement;
- a struggle the audience recognises.

With SCQA for executives, the answer is the opening.

## Data as story

When you present data:

1. Lead with the insight, not the chart.
2. Give context: "This is three times what our competitors do."
3. Make it human: "That's one customer every 12 seconds."
4. Use a comparison the audience can picture.

## Presentation structure

When the result is a presentation, put the chosen framework into this structure:

1. **Hook** (30 seconds): grab attention at once.
2. **Stakes** (1 to 2 minutes): why this matters.
3. **Journey** (most of the time): the framework, with tension and resolution.
4. **Call to action:** one specific ask.
5. **Memorable close:** circle back to the opening, or end with the vision.

## Output

Give the result in the format the user asked for: an outline, a script, a document or slide texts. Write it in the language of the audience. After the result, give in a few lines:

- the framework and why;
- the assumptions you made, if any;
- the placeholders that still need real facts, if any.

If the user wants slides in a specific tool, deliver the story first. If a skill for that tool is available, such as `ia-presenter`, use it for the file.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with company history or credentials.
- Burying the point at the end.
- Data without interpretation.
- No clear ask or next step.
- Making yourself the hero instead of the audience or the customer.
- Abstract language where a concrete example exists.
