# Rhetoric and Presentation Structure

Read this file when helping users structure presentations, develop arguments, or improve their storytelling.

## The Five Canons of Rhetoric

These principles have guided speakers for over two thousand years. They're not a checklist but a cycle.

### 1. Idea (Inventio)

The heart of a great presentation is the message, not how it looks.

**Key questions:**
- Who is the audience?
- What is the purpose?
- What do I want them to feel, think, or do?

Focus on content first. Slides are visual aids to storytelling, not the essence. Connection comes through the power of voice, not stock images or bullet points.

> "They may forget what you said, but they will never forget how you made them feel." —attributed to Carl W. Buechner

### 2. Structure (Dispositio)

A clear flow keeps the audience engaged. Structure is the framework that gives your message order.

**Starting structure (Rule of Threes):**

1. **Introduction** - Set the stage. Explain the problem. Show why they should care. Give a quick overview.

2. **Development** - The main part. Present your argument. Each point connects to the next. Ideas flow logically.

3. **Conclusion** - Summarize key points. Reinforce the core message. Leave something to remember. Finish strong.

**Principles:**

- **One idea per slide.** Too many ideas confuse. Simple slides make messages clearer.
- **Logical flow.** Whether timeline or problem-solution, the audience should always know where they are.
- **Repetition for impact.** Repeat key points to make them stick. Don't overdo it.
- **Timing and pacing.** Don't rush. Let each slide breathe. Give the audience time to absorb.

**On restructuring:** Use the rule of threes to start, then optimize. But don't shuffle slides randomly hoping they make sense. Think and rethink structure consciously.

### 3. Style (Elocutio)

Find the right words before the right picture. Think about the right metaphor before the right image.

**Words:**
- Simplify sentences
- Find connecting words
- Speak the audience's language
- The right words are not the loudest or most flowery

**Visuals:**
- Strong headlines and great images reinforce what you're saying
- They give a visual hook to hang the idea on
- Not every image is worth a thousand words
- Stock imagery insults intelligence

**Creating tension:** Inexperienced presenters choose images that duplicate the message. Experienced presenters choose images that raise questions. Show something that makes them want to listen for the explanation.

**Typography:**
- Fewer words = less worry about typography
- Avoid walls of text (the #1 presentation killer)
- Billboard test: readable at a glance
- 5-5-5 guideline: max 5 lines, max 5 words per line, max 5 consecutive text-heavy slides

**On bullet points:** They increase cognitive load, make you predictable, and should be your notes, not your slides. If you must list items, consider giving each its own slide.

### 4. Memory (Memoria)

Practice three times minimum for important presentations. Two things happen:

**Making it your own:** You experience your own argument. Hear your own voice. Notice snags and rough edges. Smooth them out. This is deliberate practice.

**Easing anxiety:** Uncertainty fades the more you engage with the script. You're laying down memories, one on top of another, calming your nervous system.

The goal: know your slides so well you won't need the teleprompter. But it's nice knowing it's there.

### 5. Delivery (Actio)

Speaker notes are your safety net, like karaoke subtitles. You already know the song. The words are there if you get lost.

**Principles:**
- When choosing between longer or shorter: pick shorter
- "I wish you had talked longer" are six words you'll seldom hear
- Finish on time
- Convey only the most important points

**On fear:** Being afraid is normal. Even experienced speakers know that fear. Deal with it by being prepared enough that part of you looks forward to presenting. Be so prepared you're the most informed person in the room on your topic.

## Conclusions

The conclusion is not a formality. It's the moment of maximum clarity, persuasion, and emotional impact. How you end often decides what the audience remembers.

### Three Types

**Recapitulation** - Repeat the main headings of the speech. Useful after long speeches. Like choosing a small package from your large assortment for the audience to carry away.

**Summary** - Condense the entire material into shortened, general statements. Doesn't repeat previous statements, just recalls them. More common than recapitulation.

**Peroration** - Appeals to feelings and emotions. Strives to move the audience to act. Usually comes at the end of persuasive speeches. Appeals to right, justice, humanity, religion.

### Direction

**Retrospective** - Looks back at what was said. Emphasizes, repeats, clarifies, enforces. Common in explanatory addresses.

**Anticipatory** - Looks forward to what may happen, what must be done, what votes should be cast. More frequent because most speeches anticipate future action.

**Both** - Can look backward and forward. The division need not be equal. The effect on the audience determines the balance.

### On Length

The shorter the better, if consistent with clarity and effect. A brief remark cuts deeper than a long involved sentence. Short speeches require short conclusions. Long speeches more naturally require longer ones.

## Opening Your Presentation

Don't worry too much about first words when starting to prepare. Just start. Once you've written your story and know what you'll say, the intro comes easily. The best openings come at the end of preparation.

**Options:**
- Start with what everyone knows and agrees
- Start with something shocking to get attention
- Dive right in (pretend you already introduced yourself)
- Introduce yourself humbly and praise the audience

Skip the agenda slide. Start with why anyone should care.

## Business Storytelling Frameworks

For business presentations, these structures complement the Five Canons:

**SCQA (Situation-Complication-Question-Answer)**
- Situation: The stable state everyone accepts
- Complication: What changed or went wrong
- Question: The issue this raises
- Answer: Your proposal

**Problem-Solution-Benefit**
- Problem: What's broken
- Solution: How to fix it
- Benefit: Why it matters (quantified if possible)

**Minto Pyramid**
- Lead with the conclusion
- Group supporting arguments
- Order logically within groups

**Before-After-Bridge**
- Before: The painful current state
- After: The better future
- Bridge: How to get there
