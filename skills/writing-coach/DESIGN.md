# Writing coach: design and rationale

This document explains why the skill works the way it does. For each choice it states what was decided, why, what it rests on, and which alternative was rejected. Source references appear as [S1], [S2] and so on. The source list at the bottom says for each source what was opened.

Written 24 October 2026. Revised the same day: outline and paragraphs as loops ended by the user, proposals only on request, and a content check in the final round (choices 5, 9 and 11). Revised again the same day: a budget in words or time (choice 12).

## Goal

You write reports and articles that you publish or hand in under your own name. The coach helps you decide what you want to say, build a structure that carries it, find sources that hold up, and get feedback that tells you what to do next. You write every part yourself first. You hand over the work that doesn't need your judgment: background research, a proposal when you are stuck, and the final polish. At the end, the coach checks how well your positions and conclusions hold up. The text stays yours.

## Choice 1: A coach, not a ghostwriter

**Decision.** You set the point, the questions and the outline, and you make the first attempt at every paragraph. The coach writes a proposal only when you ask, marks it, and never rewrites your paragraphs unasked.

**Why.**
- **AI saves time on writing tasks.** Professionals with ChatGPT finished short writing tasks 40% faster, with 18% higher rated quality. The facts in those texts were not checked [S1]. So handing over work is worth it, but not without checking.
- **Doing it for you can cost skill.** Pupils who practised maths with unrestricted GPT-4 did 17% worse afterwards without it. A version that gave hints instead of answers largely prevented this [S2]. That is maths, not writing; the parallel is a derivation. A small EEG study on essay writing with ChatGPT points the same way, but it is a preprint with criticised methods [S3], so it carries no weight on its own.
- **Ownership.** People feel less ownership of AI-generated text, and more when they had more influence on it [S4]. For a text under your name, ownership is the point.
- **Sameness.** Stories written with AI ideas were rated more creative, but were more alike [S5]. An article that should show your view gains little from that.
- **Design matters.** An AI tutor built on teaching principles (try first, small steps, no ready answers) gave more learning in less time than active learning in class [S6]. The coach follows the same principles.

**Rejected.** Generating a full draft from a brief and editing it afterwards. Faster, but it puts the AI's structure and phrasing first, and that is exactly what [S4] and [S5] warn about.

## Choice 2: Try first, then options

**Decision.** For the point, the questions and the outline, you try first. The coach asks questions. Only after a real attempt, or when you ask, does it offer two or three options to choose from or combine. If you are stuck after one question and one hint, it switches to options.

**Why.** Hints instead of answers protect learning [S2]; the tutor in [S6] also let learners try first. Offering several options instead of one keeps the choice with you. This is consistent with the `active-learning` skill.

**Limitation.** Under a tight deadline this is slower. You can ask for options straight away; the coach saves that as a decision for the project.

## Choice 3: Point, reader and your own material first

**Decision.** Before any research, you write the point in one sentence, describe one reader, and list in keywords what you already know and think.

**Why.** Experienced writers transform what they know to reach a goal, and the most skilled also weigh what the reader will understand [S7]. Experts systematically overestimate what others know (the curse of knowledge) [S8], so the reader has to be made explicit. Writing your own material down before sources come in is a derivation from [S5]: what you see first steers what you think.

## Choice 4: Main question and sub-questions

**Decision.** A report gets one main question, split into sub-questions that are each needed and together sufficient. An article gets the questions the reader will ask, in their order. For an opinion piece, the strongest objection is always a sub-question.

**Why.** "Each necessary, together sufficient" is the standard design rule in Dutch practice-oriented research [S9]. The test for overlap and gaps (MECE) and the opening frame situation-complication-question-answer come from Minto's pyramid principle, widely used in consultancy [S10]. These are practice methods, not research findings. The objection as a fixed sub-question rests on research: two-sided messages that refute the other side persuade more than one-sided ones, and two-sided messages that don't refute persuade less [S11].

## Choice 5: The outline as a loop, before any paragraph

**Decision.** Phase 5 produces only the outline: concrete headings and one core sentence per paragraph that states a claim. You write an attempt, the coach gives feedback, you refine. The loop repeats until you say the outline is good enough. The coach never writes the whole outline; if you are stuck, it offers options for that part only.

**Why.**
- Writing an outline first improved text quality in experiments with students, and a mental outline worked as well as a written one: the gain comes from planning, not from the paper [S12, S13]. Planning strategies and prewriting gave small to medium gains in school studies [S14].
- Core sentences as claims make the check possible: if they don't carry the argument on their own, the text won't either.
- Repeated rounds of feedback and revision are part of the practices that worked in school studies; writing more without them did not help [S14].
- You end the loop, not the coach. Hattie and Timperley give self-assessment an important place in feedback [S16], and people feel more ownership of a text the more influence they have on it [S4]. The coach may name a remaining problem once.

## Choice 6: Research in the background, with cards you can check

**Decision.** As soon as the sub-questions are set, research starts in the background, per sub-question. Every finding becomes a card with the source, the type of study, who was studied, the size of the effect, the limits and what was actually opened. Only opened sources are cited.

**Why.**
- **Made-up references are real.** In generated literature reviews, 55% (GPT-3.5) and 18% (GPT-4) of references were fabricated, and many real ones had errors [S15]. So the coach opens every source, and record what was opened.
- **Strength of evidence.** Meta-analyses first, preprints labelled, population and effect size recorded. This follows from how the overview article was built and from the limits it names.
- **In the background** so you keep working on the outline. Where background agents are not available, research runs in a turn of its own while you get a task.
- **Contradicting findings change the plan.** A finding that challenges the point is reported first and leads to a decision: adjust the point, add the objection, or narrow the claim.

## Choice 7: Feedback that ends with a next step

**Decision.** Feedback says what the paragraph should do, what works and what doesn't, and ends with one concrete next step. At most three points per round. Content and structure before sentences and words. Questions for content, direct advice for form.

**Why.**
- Feedback should answer three questions: where am I going, how am I going, where to next [S16]. In an analysis of feedback on student essays, "where to next" feedback was the most powerful [S17].
- Across 435 studies, feedback had a medium average effect (d = 0.48), with large differences; the information content mattered most [S18]. That is why the feedback is specific, tied to the brief and the outline, not general praise.
- "Higher-order concerns before lower-order concerns" and "questions for content, direct advice for form" are long-standing writing-center practice. The evidence for them is thin: in one study, tutors and students preferred direct advice for form and questions for content [S19]. Treat it as a sensible default.
- At most three points is a practice rule to keep feedback usable.

## Choice 8: AI feedback is not enough; a reader test

**Decision.** Before publication, the coach suggests one reader from the target audience: where did you lose interest, and what do you think the point is?

**Why.** Automated writing feedback had a medium effect (g = 0.55) in a meta-analysis of 20 studies, with large differences, and the authors advise combining it with human feedback [S20]. A later meta-analysis of 33 studies found a smaller effect (g = 0.36), and no significant effect for ChatGPT feedback specifically [S21]. One real reader catches what the coach misses.

## Choice 9: Paragraphs as a loop, with plain-writing as the yardstick

**Decision.** You make the first attempt at every paragraph. The coach gives feedback, you refine, and the loop repeats until you say the paragraph is good enough. The `plain-writing` skill is the yardstick for the feedback on sentences, words and paragraphs, and the basis for every proposal. You can ask for a proposal at any time; the coach writes it in your voice from the outline, the source cards and your own material, marks it, and asks you to make it your own. That version counts as your attempt.

**Why.**
- Hints and feedback instead of ready answers protect skill [S2]; a well-designed AI tutor lets learners try first [S6].
- Feedback with a clear next step works best [S17]; specific information matters most [S18]. One set of writing rules, maintained in one place, keeps the feedback consistent.
- A proposal you rewrite yourself keeps ownership with you [S4] and keeps your phrasing in the text instead of the AI's [S5].

**Rejected.** Assigning paragraphs to the coach in the outline. It saves time, but those paragraphs would never pass through your own attempt.

## Choice 10: Works in Claude and ChatGPT, no scripts

**Decision.** As in `active-learning`: the open SKILL.md standard, instructions only, and all platform differences in `references/platform.md`. Status and sources live in the platform's storage; the draft lives where you can edit it.

**Why.** You want to continue on several devices and platforms. Instructions work everywhere; scripts don't.

## Choice 11: A content check in the final round

**Decision.** Together with the polish, the coach checks the content. For every main position and conclusion it names how likely it is given the evidence (well supported, plausible, contested or unsupported), with the reason and the sources. It names the assumptions the text makes without saying so, and flags conclusions that claim more than the evidence carries, such as findings from pupils used for managers, correlation as cause, or one case as a rule. It also names counter-evidence from the sources file that the text does not address. The coach doesn't change content; you decide per point.

**Why.**
- The polish only touches form. The biggest risk for a text under your name is a claim that doesn't hold up, and that risk is not caught by sentence-level rules. In the experiment on AI-assisted professional writing, the facts were not checked at all [S1].
- Stating uncertainty costs little trust, especially as a range in numbers [S22]. A check that labels each position makes it easy to say once where the evidence is thin.
- Answering the strongest objection persuades more than ignoring it [S11]; naming unaddressed counter-evidence gives you the chance to do that.
- The four labels and the list of overreach patterns are a practice tool, derived from how the overview article handled its own evidence. They are not a validated scale.

## Choice 12: A budget in words or time

**Decision.** The intake asks for a budget before any other work: words, time, both or none. A word budget sets how many paragraphs and sections fit, and gives every paragraph in the outline a word target. A time budget goes through a reality check: the work split into steps, a range per paragraph, planned back from the deadline, and three lists (fits, almost fits, out of reach). After three paragraphs, the measured pace replaces the starting guess. With a small budget, the steps shrink, but you still write first and still end each loop.

**Why.**
- **The budget decides the plan.** The number of sub-questions and paragraphs follows from the length; a plan made without it has to be cut later. That is why it is asked first. This is a design choice, like the same question in `active-learning`.
- **People underestimate how long tasks take.** Students predicted 34 days for their thesis; it took 55 [S23]. Splitting a task into subtasks makes estimates less optimistic [S24], and planning back from the deadline gave more realistic predictions than planning forwards [S25].
- **Measure instead of remember.** People also underestimate afterwards how long earlier tasks took [S26]. That is why the coach asks the time at the end of each session, and uses your own pace after three paragraphs.
- **No fixed buffer.** As in `active-learning`: no percentage has a basis for this kind of work, so the coach plans with the high end of the range and corrects with what it measures.

**Limitation.** The sizes are practice rules, not research findings: about 10% for the opening and the close, 100 to 150 words per paragraph, at least two paragraphs per section, and the starting guess of 20 to 45 minutes per paragraph. The sources [S23] to [S26] were not reopened for this skill; they come from the rationale of `active-learning`, which records what was checked.

## Limits

- Most of the writing research behind these choices was done with school pupils and students. The persuasion research is mostly about health messages. Effects are mostly small to medium.
- Several choices are practice rules or derivations, and are marked as such: the outline of core sentences, three points per round, your own material before research, the polish limits, the labels of the content check, and the sizes in the budget.
- No study tested this combination of steps. Whether it works for you shows in use; adjust the skill when it doesn't.
